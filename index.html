JustRUA

A Controlled Study of GPT-6 Robot-Use Agents

Zefan Wang*†, Yufan Dang*, Xuanwei Shi*, Chuan Gao*, Cheng Yin, SikYuen Tam, Hanyu Liu, Yasheng Wang, Yaxi Lu, Yi Jiang, Songyang Zhang, Xin Cong, Yuan Yao‡

*Core contributor†Project lead‡Corresponding author

GPT-6 Astra can drive a robot arm directly through a fixed harness, and scores on RoboDojo have risen quickly since. Reported systems bring many improvements that may help, but they may start from different harnesses, use different test subsets and change several parts of the design at once, which can make it hard to tell which choices produce most of the gains. We take these designs apart and study them in one framework. JustRUA starts from the official L3 harness and keeps only what matters most. With no learned policy, no self-improvement loop and no task-specific tuning, this minimal harness raises its score on the RoboDojo simulation benchmark from 29.7 to 50.3.

Revising the prompts lifts the first round from 54.0 to 55.6. The rest would need trial and error on each task, which comes close to tuning on the test tasks themselves.

Controlled study framework

Scores reported on RoboDojo differ in harness and test subset, and most reports change several parts of the design at once. Reproducing each one would carry those differences over. We instead take the designs apart and test them on one testbed and one benchmark, changing as little as we can between runs.

Blue outlines mark the parts JustRUA changes from the official L3 harness. Select a dashed area to see what we varied there.

01Harness and action

Our starting point was a discrepancy. The first evaluation of GPT-6 Astra on RoboDojo found it weak on tasks that require precision, scoring 12.7 on Precision, one of RoboDojo's five task groups, under the official L3 harness (RoboProbe), and a broader study from Galbot saw a gap between its task decisions and reliable physical control (Galbot). Yet a few reasonable fixes to the harness and the action loop, with no elaborate design on top, brought its Precision score to the level of the strongest vision-language-action models of the time (25.0 on our evaluation set, against 24.8 for DM0.5). We came to suspect that much of the weakness lay in the harness rather than in the model.

1.1Codex and full history

The official L3 harness, L3 Inspect EEF from RoboProbe, calls the model API directly and keeps images from the last two observations only. We run the same loop in a Codex session* that keeps every image of the episode, with Codex's own tools switched off. The controlled comparisons keep the harness's default cap of 100 decisions per episode, and only the final configuration of JustRUA removes it. Moving the loop into Codex alone lowered the first-round score, so we credit the gain to Codex and the full history together.

1.2Action design

Motion length. CuRobo occasionally returns an unstable plan, and in the official loop the whole motion runs before the model sees anything. We cap each motion at 20 control steps, so the model looks again before a bad plan carries the arm far. We also tried letting the model choose 3 to 32 steps per motion, as in action chunking (ACT, Astranomy). JustRUA keeps the fixed cap.

Alternative interfaces. Two alternatives come from recent systems, and neither beat absolute targets. Relative targets give position and orientation as offsets from the measured pose, as in RoboDawn. They scored lower, much as Astranomy found, and the arm struck objects almost three times as often, 20 times against 7 in the first round. One likely reason is that every coordinate the model reads, from the task recipe to the measured pose, is given in the world frame, so an offset asks for one more subtraction before each move.

Semantic operations replace raw targets with named skills, a common way to connect language models to robots (SayCan, Code as Policies). Ours are built on move_eef and only describe moves at a higher level, with no new control code or learned policy. Some name the purpose of a move, such as approach or transport, some merge several moves into one call, such as pick, and adjust_view comes with guidance to look before acting. They changed what the model did more than how well it did it. It moved its camera more than three times as often and dropped more of what it held, and the score did not rise.

02Reusing experience

Experience from other episodes is the core of recursive self-improvement, and of everything we tried it carries the greatest risk of gaming the benchmark. Looking up a task's own demonstration comes close to routing tasks to separate policies, and practising on the test layouts comes close to training on the test set. We tested it because it is widely used. No form of experience clearly beat the default.

The visual demo and the text trajectory both draw on one expert-curated successful episode per task from RoboDawn, looked up by task name and inserted once before the first decision. The visual demo shows the 16 keyframes its author selected. A better plan is not enough. With either demonstration the model passed objects through the table more often, 15 and 13 times against 9 in the first round, and dropped fewer. In make_kong, without a demonstration it never hands the tile to the other arm, as the error analysis shows. With either demonstration it copies the handover in the demonstration. The text trajectory carries this handover only as coordinates, and the model still found it. Neither run copies the last step, where the demonstration grips the tile upright to push it into the row, and both lose the tiles on that push and score 0. Fixes of this kind are lost to failures elsewhere, so the overall score barely moves.

2.2Self-improvement loop

Several RoboDojo entries improve themselves by writing and refining their own skills (EmbodiedEvo, SenseNova-RoboRSI). Here a Codex agent writes extra operations for the model, as Python code built on move_eef, together with a guide on when to use them. It starts from the RoboDawn demonstrations of all 42 tasks. The tasks are split into five groups. In each round the model runs the current library on one group, and the agent then reviews those runs, with their scores, action logs and videos, and revises the library for the next round. Nothing is edited by hand. Unlike the demonstrations, the result is not stored or looked up by task.

03Scene geometry

Recent agent systems feed the model depth, point clouds and affordances from dedicated models. We asked how much of this GPT-6 actually needs. Its own grip readings, depth predicted from RGB and a little code that turns pixels into 3D coordinates go a long way without any simulator ground truth, while precomputed affordances, the most elaborate piece, did not help and were left out.

3.1Robot readings

Robot readings are the basic sensor values of each gripper, finger positions and forces, contact force and wrist load. They carry no privileged information, such as the true pose of any object in the simulator.

Their clearest use is in grasping. The default interface reports the jaw opening the model asked for, so after closing on an object the model sees its own target. The readings give the opening the fingers actually reached and the force at each fingertip.

3.2Depth

        
We further add depth. Many recent agent systems use depth to place objects in 3D (RoboHarness, GPT-GEAR, RAX), but the RoboDojo leaderboard does not accept depth read from the simulator for agent entries, as a maintainer stated in review. We therefore test both, depth from the simulator and depth that Depth Pro predicts from the RGB image alone, against the robot readings of the previous step.

        
The predicted distances are far off. Correcting the focal length still leaves errors of tens of centimetres. A scale fitted to the robot's own geometry in each frame brings them down to a few centimetres, but that scale drifts between frames and cannot be fitted for the wrist cameras, so JustRUA passes the raw predictions on.

3.3Affordances

        
Depth tells the model where surfaces are, but not where an object can be held or which arm can get to it. This kind of knowledge is an affordance, what an object or a place offers a robot, such as a handle that can be grasped or a stretch of table that one arm can reach. Recent agent systems compute affordances with dedicated models and hand them to the planner (CLARO, ORA). Since many episodes go wrong at the grasp, we tested whether giving the model this knowledge directly would help.

        
We compute two kinds of affordance for each camera view. A pretrained grasp generator (GraspGen) proposes up to eight grasp poses on the point cloud of each object, and inverse kinematics tests which surfaces each arm can reach with its current tool orientation. Both are drawn onto the camera image. The text lists the grasp poses as positions and angles, along with hints such as which candidates a camera cannot see. The real difficulty lies in presenting all this to the model. Each observation already carries the camera views and their depth, and anything added to it either takes more space or crowds what is already there.

Against depth images from the same source, none of the four versions grasped more reliably or scored higher. The moments where affordances could make a difference are also few. Reach matters most when one arm cannot get to an object and hands it to the other, and handovers stayed rare in every version, the panel included. Grasp candidates matter only in the decisions that start a grasp. The cost, in contrast, comes with every observation, since keeping the affordances in view means either adding images and tokens or shrinking the other views. We weighed the two and left affordances out of JustRUA.

3.4Coordinate solving

        
In the last step we try to unlock the full potential of GPT-6 working in Codex. The model may now write and run code in its session1, as in Code as Policies and, more recently, URAI, and the 100-decision cap that all earlier comparisons keep is lifted.

        
The model used code in a consistent way. Almost every run solves for coordinates, casting rays from pixels through the camera calibration and then triangulating a point seen by two cameras or meeting a known surface height. The code works from the images and text the model already receives and never reads the depth images. The few runs that open files only list the inputs or reread the model's own helper code. Code therefore acts as one more source of scene geometry, which is why we place it on this axis.

04Error analysis

      
With our best configuration, 21 of the 42 first-round tasks score below 50. Whether these failures share a cause decides what further gains would take, one more general fix or work on each task. We reviewed every such episode and sorted them by cause. Some fail for more than one reason, and most turn out to be specific to one task.


      

      
4.1Failure classification

      

        

        

        

        

      

      


      

        
4.2Potential fixes

        
Most of these failures are long-tail. Each of the first three causes points to a general direction, but each needs knowledge that is costly to supply.

        

          
StrategyPlanning with high-level knowledge of the arm, such as which flip angles one arm cannot reach while placing. Which limits matter changes from task to task.

          
CollisionCollision checking against complete digital assets of the arm and of every object it holds. Modelling every held object and joint at that level is hardly feasible.

          
Unstable graspGrasp planning on the same digital assets, to model how each irregular or round object sits in the gripper.

        

        
The prompt and scoring cases are easier. Revising the task prompts raised the first-round score from 54.0 to 55.6. The other failures are hard to remove without trial and error on each task, which comes close to tuning on the test tasks themselves.

      

      
    


    

      
Notes on evaluation

      
The official RoboDojo evaluation scores each task over 50 episodes, since a task's score on any single layout is spiky and one run can mislead. We run far fewer episodes, so which layouts we run matters. Each round runs the next layout of every task in a fixed order, and rounds are not equally hard. Every configuration in this report therefore runs on the same fixed layouts, in the first round and on the evaluation set alike, rather than on fresh random draws.


      

        
Round-to-round variation

        
Low and medium effort rise and fall together across eight rounds, with a correlation of 0.84, although we have not separated layout difficulty from run-to-run randomness. A score from the first round alone tends to run high. In all three settings in the table, the first round scored above the mean of eight rounds. For the official L3 harness the eight-round mean, 28.7, is close to the full 2,100-episode score of 29.0, while the first round reads 32.3.
